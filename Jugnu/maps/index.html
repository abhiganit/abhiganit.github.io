A world atlas for a child who cannot read yet. Turn on one thing at a time — mountains, or rivers, or volcanoes — tap any of it, and the map says out loud what it is and how big.

The atlas

Pick one layer. Drag to move, scroll or pinch to zoom, tap anything to hear it. More appears as you zoom in — a handful of mountains across the whole world, hundreds once you are down over the Himalaya. The search box finds any of the six hundred peaks, fifteen hundred volcanoes, a thousand rivers and three thousand towns underneath.

For the grown-up

How to use it with a small child

  • One layer at a time. The whole point is that the map is never busy.
  • Start with the place you live. Search for your town, then zoom out slowly until it disappears into the country. That move — the zoom itself — is what teaches scale.
  • Mountains are drawn to size. Everest and the hill at the end of your road are the same kind of object, and the triangles say which is which before any number does.
  • The ? button asks for a place out loud and waits for a tap. Wrong answers get a hint, not a buzzer.
  • Pins you add in My places stay in this browser. Nothing is sent anywhere, and nothing here needs an account.

Where the data comes from

  • Coastlines, countries and US states: Natural Earth, public domain.
  • 632 named peaks with elevations, including their Hindi names, and about a thousand named rivers: also Natural Earth.
  • 1,558 volcanoes: the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program's Holocene list, via NOAA. The eruption dates are grouped into bands, and this copy of the list is old enough that recent eruptions are missing. Treat "last erupted" as roughly right, not current.
  • Around 3,300 towns and cities for the search box.
  • The map is a plain equirectangular projection. It stretches Greenland and Antarctica badly, as every flat world map does something badly. Worth saying out loud once a child is old enough to notice.
